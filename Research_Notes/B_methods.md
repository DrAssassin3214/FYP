### B01 - Herrerias-Velasco, Herrerias-Pleguezuelo, van Dorp (2011)
- Title: Revisiting the PERT mean and variance
- Journal: European Journal of Operational Research, vol. 210, pp. 448-451 (issue NR)
- DOI: 10.1016/j.ejor.2010.08.014 (verified via CrossRef: title, journal, year, volume, pages, three authors matched)
- Access: FT of the authors' version (van Dorp's GWU page; running header cites EJOR 210, 448-451). Free copy, licence NR - not declared OA.
- Context: NON-CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGICAL (PERT theory, expert-judgement three-point estimates).
- Key equations / parameters (as printed; the PDF typeset is partly garbled in extraction):
  - Classical PERT (eq. 2): E[X|a,m,b] = (a + 4m + b)/6 with variance ((b-a)/6)^2 - the clean typeset forms are confirmed in B02 and B04 (a = min, m = mode, b = max).
  - delta = (m - a)/(b - a) is the relative position of the elicited mode (eq. 7); m = (1 - delta)a + delta*b (eq. 8).
  - Beta shape parameterisation: p = 3 -/+ h, q = 3 +/- h (sign garbled in extraction), h(delta) = 2 - 4*delta as I read eq. (10). The paper states the family keeps the PERT mean expression and makes the beta mode equal the elicited m.
  - Proposed variance: V[X|a,m,b] = ((b-a)^2 / 36) * R(delta) (eq. 11), where R is a "PERT variance adjustment factor". Printed properties of R: minimum 5/7 at delta in {0,1}; maximum 9/7 at delta = 1/2; R = 1 exactly when (1 - delta)*delta = 1/8; R > 1 (more conservative than classical PERT) for delta in about [0.15, 0.85].
  - INFERENCE (mine, not printed in readable form): R(delta) = (5 + 16*delta*(1 - delta))/7. This matches every printed anchor (5/7, 9/7, R = 1 at delta(1 - delta) = 1/8) and equals the variance of Beta(alpha = 1 + 4*delta, beta = 5 - 4*delta) on [a,b] computed from the beta variance formula in B04 (alpha*beta/(k^3 + k^2) with k = 6). NC against the typeset published formula.
- Key findings: Malcolm et al. (1959) mean/variance give a beta whose mode differs from the elicited m in all but three cases (Grubbs 1962); the asymmetric members p = 3 +/- sqrt(2), q = 3 -/+ sqrt(2) have kurtosis 3.0 (normal-like; credited to Kamburowski 1997 and Herrerias et al. 2003 - secondary, not read). The constant-variance-given-range property of PERT (Hahn 2008 concern) is addressed by letting variance depend on m.
- Limitations: theoretical note only; no data, no construction application, no numerical validation.
- Decision supported: "Beta-PERT with lambda = 4" is the alpha + beta = 6, mode-preserving family (see baseline section); PERT variance is not a free parameter; variance is larger than classical PERT when the mode is central.

### B02 - Trietsch, Mazmanyan, Gevorgyan, Baker (2012)
- Title: Modeling activity times by the Parkinson distribution with a lognormal core: Theory and validation
- Journal: European Journal of Operational Research 216(2?), pp. 386-396 (issue NR; volume 216 and pages 386-396 verified)
- DOI: 10.1016/j.ejor.2011.07.054 (verified via CrossRef)
- Access: FT of the authors' working paper dated October 2010 (Tuck School site), NOT the published version. Free copy, licence NR.
- Context: project-scheduling data from several field sources, including one construction project in Yerevan, Armenia (107 activities) and residential/community/agricultural/software projects. Partly construction; NOT construction-only.
- Key equations (as printed):
  - PERT: mu = (min + 4*mode + max)/6 (1); sigma = (max - min)/6 (2). (2) "was selected arbitrarily, to resemble a truncated normal between +/-2.96 sigma".
  - PERT-beta is a good approximation only if mode lies between min + 0.13(max - min) and min + 0.87(max - min), which implies cv below about 0.66; eqs (1)-(2) imply cv <= 1.
  - Lognormal moments: mu_j = exp(m_j + s_j^2/2); sigma_j^2 = mu_j^2 [exp(s_j^2) - 1] (3). Sum of lognormals approximated by lognormal: s_X^2 = ln(1 + cv_X^2), m_X = ln(mu_X) - s_X^2/2 (4) (the printed m_X line is garbled; I wrote the standard form). Lognormal central limit theorem cited (Mazmanyan et al. 2008 - not read).
  - Linear association: Y_j = B*X_j, with X_j independent positive and B a common positive bias/productivity factor independent of X_j; if X_j and B are lognormal, Y_j is lognormal.
  - Parkinson effect: fraction p_P of early activities is recorded as exactly on time; Blom scores z_j = Phi^{-1}[(j - 0.375)/(n(1 - p_P) + 0.25)] for the censored Q-Q fit of ln(p_j/e_j) (p = actual, e = estimate).
- Key findings with numbers:
  - In Malcolm et al.'s numerical examples the average cv is 0.19 and the maximum 0.29, i.e. triplet estimates give very low cv; observations by Tversky & Kahneman (1974), cited in the paper, suggest experts' 98% intervals are exceeded in roughly 30% of instances (secondary citation, original not read).
  - Yerevan construction project: 107 points, 20 strictly tardy, 38 exactly on time; estimated p_P = 38/(107 - 20) = 0.437; Q-Q R^2 0.9178 raw, 0.9726 after Parkinson correction; after trimming and tie-averaging sqrt(R^2) about 0.9936 and 0.9947 (p-values about 75%). Lognormality not rejected at 5%.
  - Five-project family: s = 0.565, 0.708, 0.725, 0.797, 0.801 (cv = 0.613, 0.806, 0.831, 0.944, 0.948); only one could plausibly be fitted by a PERT-beta. 185 activities originally, 160 after removing 25 long ones. Nine-project family: 54 activities, 18 exactly on time.
  - Independence assumption makes PERT underestimate variance of project durations; linear association explains the data much better ("results much more plausible").
- Limitations: mixed non-construction data; time scales coarse (rounding/ties); small numbers of projects (authors say five is too few); ratios actual/estimate, not delay magnitudes given an event; author working-paper version read.
- Decision supported: lognormal as an alternative or robustness distribution for continuous variability; PERT triplets tend to understate spread; a common multiplicative factor B is a defensible way to induce positive dependence.

### B03 - Trietsch & Baker (2012)
- Title: PERT 21: Fitting PERT/CPM for use in the 21st century
- Journal: International Journal of Project Management 30, pp. 490-502 (issue NR)
- DOI: 10.1016/j.ijproman.2011.09.004 (verified via CrossRef)
- Access: FT of the "accepted for publication, 29 September 2011" manuscript (Tuck site). Free copy, licence NR.
- Context: project-management DSS methodology; uses field data (incl. residential construction example in Fig. 1). NOT construction-only.
- Key statements/numbers (as printed): across the datasets analysed by Trietsch et al., lowest cv encountered about 0.37, median about 1, highest above 2; beta approximation valid only for cv <= 0.66, and 80% of tested cases violate that limit; "we found no evidence that [PERT] validity was ever tested successfully on field data". Under independence the cv of a serial project's duration tends to 0 as activity count grows, whereas under linear association it tends to the cv of B. Risk-register modules: user lists "known unknowns", assigns probability and effect (subjective); default assumption is independence; "there is no established methodology" for eliciting valid correlation coefficients; subjective 98% confidence intervals hold only about 70% of outcomes (Tversky & Kahneman 1974, secondary). Tornado diagrams "do not take cost into account". Monte Carlo simulation removes the Jensen-gap problem of PERT (Jensen gap = project duration mean exceeds the mean of the critical path because of path merging).
- Limitations: methodology proposal; validation is in companion paper (B02); no risk-event calibration reported; authors call for research on estimating risk-event probabilities and correlations from history.
- Decision supported: independence default is criticised - include a dependence option; treat risk-register probabilities as subjective and uncertain.

### B04 - Hahn (2008)
- Title: Mixture densities for project management activity times: A robust approach to PERT
- Journal: European Journal of Operational Research 188(2), pp. 450-459 (pages and volume confirmed in reference lists of B01; issue number as cited)
- DOI: 10.1016/j.ejor.2007.04.032 (verified via Semantic Scholar record with matching title, venue, year 2008)
- Access: FT of the author's working paper dated 4 February 2007 (faculty page). Free copy, licence NR.
- Context: NON-CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGICAL (illustration: a 29-activity electronic-module development network, Moder et al. 1983).
- Key equations (as printed, clean text):
  - Beta on [a,b]: p(y) = Gamma(alpha + beta)/(Gamma(alpha)Gamma(beta)) * (y - a)^(alpha-1) (b - y)^(beta-1) / (b - a)^(alpha+beta-1); with k = alpha + beta: expectation alpha/k, variance alpha*beta/(k^3 + k^2), mode (alpha - 1)/(k - 2) (on the standardised interval).
  - PERT: E(y) = (a + 4m + b)/6 (2); Var(y) = (b - a)^2/36 (3). Type I PERT beta: k = 6 (mean exact). Type II (mean and variance both exact, Grubbs 1962): alpha = beta = 4; alpha = 3 +/- sqrt(2), beta = 3 -/+ sqrt(2).
  - Beta-rectangular mixture: p(y) = theta * Beta-density + (1 - theta)/(b - a), 0 <= theta <= 1 (5); mean a + (b - a)(theta*alpha/k + (1 - theta)/2) (6); variance (b - a)^2 [theta*alpha(alpha + 1)/(k(k + 1)) + (1 - theta)/3 - (k + theta(alpha - beta))^2/(4k^2)] (7); mode a + (b - a)(alpha - 1)/(k - 2) for theta > 0 (8); PERT-mixture mean [theta(a + 4m + b) + 3(1 - theta)(a + b)]/6 (9); CDF given in appendix.
  - theta elicitation: (i) certainty score on a 0-10 or 0-100 scale divided by the maximum; (ii) odds method theta = O_beta/(O_beta + O_rect) (e.g. odds 2/1 gives theta = 2/3).
- Key findings with numbers: The PERT beta is light-tailed and its variance is constant given the range; beta densities vanish at the extremes, the mixture keeps density (1 - theta)/(b - a) at the bounds. Kamburowski (1997), cited, gives kurtosis 3 for k = 6 (secondary). Electronic-module project (29 activities): mean project time 47.8, 49.6, 51.3, 53.0 days for standard beta and theta = 0.75, 0.5, 0.25; SD 3.4, 4.6, 5.2, 5.6; 95% intervals 42.2-55.3, 42.5-60.1, 43.0-62.3, 43.6-63.9 days; P(T > 60) under standard beta "less than 0.05%" versus 2.6%, 7%, 13% for theta = 0.75, 0.5, 0.25.
- Limitations: theta values are illustrative, not fitted; no field validation; a fourth elicited parameter adds burden (B02 criticism).
- Decision supported: the beta tail is the assumption that most drives upper percentiles; offer a heavier-tailed option (beta-rectangular) or at least a tail sensitivity check; P95 is much more sensitive than P50.

### B05 - Dong & Nakayama (2020)
- Title: A tutorial on quantile estimation via Monte Carlo
- Venue: Springer book chapter in "Monte Carlo and Quasi-Monte Carlo Methods" (Springer Proceedings in Mathematics & Statistics), 2020, pp. 3-30 (CrossRef; editors not returned). Manuscript stamped 5 Mar 2020.
- DOI: 10.1007/978-3-030-43465-6_1 (verified via CrossRef: title, authors, book, year, pages matched)
- Access: FT of the authors' manuscript PDF (NJIT site). Free copy, licence NR.
- Context: NON-CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGICAL, but the running example is a project network (stochastic activity network, 5 activities, 3 paths, exponential mean-1 durations).
- Key equations (as printed):
  - Quantile xi = F^{-1}(p) = inf{y : F(y) >= p} (4). Naive MC estimator: order statistic Y_{ceil(np):n} (11); it is biased for finite n.
  - CLT: sqrt(n)[xi_hat - xi] => N(0, tau^2), tau^2 = psi^2/f^2(xi) (15)-(16), where f is the density. For naive MC psi^2 = p(1 - p) (24), so the asymptotic variance is p(1 - p)/f^2(xi). Bahadur representation xi_hat ~ xi + (p - F_hat(xi))/f(xi) (18).
  - Distribution-free (binomial) CI: choose 1 <= i1 < i2 <= n with P(Bin(n,p) < i1) + P(Bin(n,p) >= i2) <= alpha; CI = [Y_{i1:n}, Y_{i2:n}) (60), coverage at least 1 - alpha (naive MC only).
  - Consistent-variance CI: xi_hat +/- z_alpha * tau_hat/sqrt(n) (61), with f estimated by a finite difference of the empirical quantile function, eta = 1/f(xi) ~ [F^{-1}(p + delta) - F^{-1}(p - delta)]/(2 delta) (62)-(63), bandwidth delta_n = 1/sqrt(n) in their test.
  - Batching CI: b independent batches of size m = n/b; [mean of batch quantiles +/- t_{b-1,alpha} S/sqrt(b)] (64); "setting b = 10 is often a reasonable choice"; sectioning CI centres on the full-sample estimate, S'^2 = (1/(b - 1)) sum (xi_hat_{m,l} - xi_hat_n)^2 (65). Latin hypercube sampling (LHS): U*_{i,j} = (U_{i,j} + pi_j(i) - 1)/n (43).
- Key findings with numbers (SAN example, exponential durations; nominal 90% CIs; true quantiles xi = 3.58049 at p = 0.6, xi = 6.66446 at p = 0.95):
  - Table 2 (naive MC), binomial CI: n = 400: relative half-width ARHW 0.053 / coverage 0.921 (p = 0.6), 0.082 / 0.932 (p = 0.95); n = 1600: 0.026 / 0.910 and 0.038 / 0.914; n = 6400: 0.013 / 0.904 and 0.018 / 0.905. Batching CI at p = 0.95: coverage 0.666 (n = 400), 0.838 (n = 1600), 0.885 (n = 6400) - undercovers for small n. Sectioning at p = 0.95: 0.888, 0.904, 0.903. Caption says batching/sectioning use "b = n/10 batches" while the text says "b = 10"; reported as printed. "The more extreme quantile is harder to estimate" (ARHW larger at p = 0.95 than at p = 0.6 for the same n).
  - Table 1 (n = 640, 10^3 replications, MSE improvement factor vs naive MC): LHS 2.58 at p = 0.6 and 2.19 at p = 0.95 (variance 2.78e-3 vs 7.18e-3, and 2.36e-2 vs 5.15e-2); control variate 1.85 and 2.44; importance sampling 1.09 and 5.09.
- Limitations: continuous smooth inputs and 5 activities; results for Bernoulli x Beta mixtures NC; independent and identically distributed outputs assumed.
- Decision supported: report a CI for P90/P95 (binomial CI is exact-coverage and trivial to compute); the P95 needs materially more samples than the median; LHS gives only about 2x MSE reduction in that example.

### B06 - Koehler, Brown, Haneuse (2009)
- Title: On the assessment of Monte Carlo error in simulation-based statistical analyses
- Journal: The American Statistician 63(2), pp. 155-162
- DOI: 10.1198/tast.2009.0030 (verified via CrossRef: title, authors, journal, volume, issue, pages matched)
- Access: FT via PMC (NIH public-access author manuscript, PMC3337209). "PMC copyright notice"; no open licence seen - not declared OA.
- Context: NON-CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGICAL (statistics simulation studies).
- Key equations (as returned by the fetch tool): MCE(phi_hat_R) = sqrt(Var[phi_hat_R]); CLT-based estimator sqrt[(1/R) sum (phi(X_r) - phi_hat_R)^2]; jackknife and bootstrap MCE estimators; "BGP" plot: R+ = (beta_hat+ / target MCE)^2, with MCE expected linear in 1/sqrt(R).
- Key findings: survey of 223 articles: only 8 reported an MCE estimate; the most common replication count was R = 1000 (74 articles); only 5 articles used R > 10,000 (a further "5 justified their choice" was reported by the fetch tool on an earlier pass; treat as unconfirmed). Required R varies enormously by target: in an Ohio lung-cancer bootstrap example, to reach MCE 0.05, R+ was 28 (a 2.5th-percentile bound), 720, 26,651 and 96,128 (a 97.5th-percentile bound); to reach MCE 0.005 the range was 2,824 to 2,665,144. "One-size-fits-all" rules discouraged.
- Limitations: those numbers are for bootstrap CI end-points in one case-control analysis, not for schedule quantiles.
- Decision supported: justify N by a stated tolerance on the reported percentile, not by convention; tail percentiles need far more replications than central ones.

### B07 - Colson & Cooke (2017)
- Title: Cross validation for the classical model of structured expert judgment
- Journal: Reliability Engineering & System Safety 163, pp. 109-120
- DOI: 10.1016/j.ress.2017.02.003 (printed in the document)
- Access: FT, journal version PDF from Strathclyde repository. OPEN ACCESS verified: the document states CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.
- Context: NON-CONSTRUCTION METHODOLOGICAL (33 professionally contracted expert-judgment studies, 2006 - March 2015: health, environment, security, etc.).
- Key definitions/equations: "statistical accuracy" = the p-value at which one would falsely reject the hypothesis that the expert's probability assessments are statistically accurate (calibration); "information" = Shannon relative information vs a background measure; combined score = accuracy x information; performance weights (PWg global, PWi item-specific, NoOp) versus equal weights (EW); linear pooling. Averaging quantiles is equivalent to harmonic weighting of densities (proof given: hw = 2/(1/f + 1/g) evaluated at matching quantiles).
- Key findings with numbers: 33 studies, 4-21 experts and 7-17 calibration variables each (pre-2006 database: 45 studies, 4-77 experts, 5-55 calibration variables). In-sample, performance weighting outperforms equal weighting in all but 1 of 33 studies; geometric mean of combined-score ratios PWg/EW = 3.36, PWi/EW = 3.86, best-expert/EW = 1.84, PWg/best-expert = 1.83. Out-of-sample, PW has lower statistical accuracy than EW ("out-of-sample penalty"); PW accuracy reaches 75% of the EW value when the training set is 80% of the calibration variables; PW information is double EW when training >= 50%. Out-of-Sample Validity Index: PW beats EW in 26 of 33 studies; probability of >= 26 successes in 33 trials if no difference = 0.001. Harmonic weighting (equivalent to quantile averaging) is rejected as statistically accurate at 5% in 18 of 33 studies (9 at 0.1%); best-to-worst expert accuracy ratio has geometric mean 890,000. Remove-one-at-a-time cross validation is biased against PW.
- Limitations: needs calibration ("seed") questions with known answers; not construction; results are for panels of 4-21 experts.
- Decision supported: how to aggregate several experts' probabilities/ranges: linear pooling (equal or performance weights) rather than quantile averaging; performance weights are only justified when seed questions exist.

### B08 - Song & Vanhoucke (2025)
- Title: Schedule risk analysis for project control with risk interactions
- Journal: Annals of Operations Research (volume/pages NR; online 2025)
- DOI: 10.1007/s10479-025-06668-8 (verified via OpenAlex: title, authors, journal, year)
- Access: FT (56-page PDF, archived copy at d-nb.info). OPEN ACCESS verified: the article states Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0).
- Context: generic project scheduling (synthetic benchmark networks RG30 and DC1); NOT construction-specific but cites construction risk literature.
- Key equations (as printed):
  - Spontaneous occurrence: o_hh = 1 if rand(0,1) <= p_hh, else 0 (2). Propagation: o_gh = 1 if rand(0,1) <= p_gh and O_.g = 1, else 0 (3). Actual impact: I_gi = u_gi if O_.g = 1, else 0 (4) (u_gi = delay in time units when risk g occurs on activity i).
  - Combining impacts on activity i: additive TRI_i = sum_g I_gi; "reducing" TRI_i = (1 - lambda_i) sum_g I_gi; "increasing" TRI_i = (1 + lambda_i) sum_g I_gi; class chosen randomly with boundary probabilities P^R, P^A, P^I (sum to 1) (5).
  - Duration: d_i = d^U_i + TRI_i (6), with d^U_i/d^P_i lognormal (latent uncertainty), d^P_i = planned duration.
  - Sensitivity metrics (Appendix 1, with S simulation runs): CI_i = (1/S) sum_s 1(tf^s_i = 0) (17); SI_i = (1/S) sum_s [d^s_i/(d^s_i + tf^s_i)] * [D^s/E(D)] (18); SSI_i = (sigma_i/sigma) * CI_i (19)-(20); CRI(r), CRI(rho) (Spearman), CRI(tau) (Kendall) between d_i and project duration D (21)-(23), CRI(rho) = 1 - 6 sum delta^2/(S(S^2 - 1)); MOI_i = (sigma_i^2/sigma_max^2) * 1/(1 + E(tf_i) - #S_i/n) (24).
- Parameter settings used in their experiments: latent uncertainty lognormal with mu' = 0.0594 and sigma' = 0.2679 ("applied in previous research", Martens & Vanhoucke 2019; Vaseghi & Vanhoucke 2023 - secondary); P^R, P^A, P^I = 0.3, 0.4, 0.3 and lambda = 0.5; risk spontaneous and transition probabilities uniform on (0.1, 0.7); risk impact triangular f2(c,d,e) with parameters following Creemers et al. 2014 (values NR here). Stopping rule (from Wang et al. 2019, 2020b, secondary): stop when the difference in mean project duration between consecutive simulations is below 0.1, with a minimum of 3000 runs (Fig. 4: mean duration of a 50-activity, high-density network stabilises after about 3000 runs). Dataset: 19,200 (RG30 x parameters) + 17,280 (DC1) instances, 30 and 50 activities.
- Key findings: CI and SI performed worst in identifying sensitive activities; CRI (r, rho, tau), SSI, MOI comparable; the proposed risk contribution index (RCI) was best when corrective actions were applied, more so as risk-network density increased; hybrid preventive/interventive strategy best. Structural point: risk events are controllable variability; "latent uncertainty" (Cleden 2017, a book, not read) is not reducible by action. The paper states additive summing is "mostly used in the literature" (Tsetlin & Winkler 2005, secondary, not read).
- Limitations: synthetic networks, generic parameters (uniform/triangular) not calibrated to construction; assumes each risk event affects only one activity; parameter values (lambda = 0.5, 0.3/0.4/0.3) are experimental settings, not empirical estimates.
- Decision supported: (a) T = latent variability + discrete risk-event delays as separate terms; (b) conditional "trigger" probabilities for risk chains; (c) additive combination default with optional reducing/increasing scenarios; (d) sensitivity ranking by correlation-type indices; (e) mean-stability stopping rule with a minimum of 3000 runs (a rule for the mean, not for P90/P95).

### B09 - Galvez & Capuz-Rizo (2016)
- Title: Assessment of global sensitivity analysis methods for project scheduling
- Journal: Computers & Industrial Engineering 93, pp. 110-120
- DOI: 10.1016/j.cie.2015.12.010 (printed in the document)
- Access: FT of the accepted manuscript from the UPV repository (riunet). Free copy, licence NR.
- Context: project scheduling with a dependency structure matrix (product-development processes), not construction; NON-CONSTRUCTION but a project-duration model.
- Key findings with numbers: methods compared: PCC/PRCC, SRC/SRRC, Morris, Sobol'-Jansen. "Standardized regression coefficients as well as the Morris and Sobol' methods were the most appropriate." In the case study SRC and total Sobol'-Jansen indices gave "virtually identical" rankings. Sobol'-Jansen cost is (n + 2) * s model evaluations (n inputs, sample size s): 350,000 calls for 5 inputs at s = 50,000, and 6,500,000 calls for the larger example. - Key findings: "based on empirical data for construction projects, the statistics of activity delays is better represented by a lognormal distribution" than by PERT-style triangular or beta (stated as a citation to the companion paper; I did not see that data). The power-law duration distribution matters for the project delay estimate; the total-float distribution matters less. With this model the predicted delay-tail P05 for two example projects was about 42 and 13 years, and about 10 years for most projects (as printed; these are very large, consistent with large multi-year projects).
- Limitations: large-project schedules (>= 1,000 activities), planned-duration-driven delay model (delay likelihood depends on planned duration, not on causes); parameter values come from a paper I did not read; not brick masonry.
- Decision supported: a "zero delay with probability 1 - p, otherwise lognormal delay" mixture is a published, empirically-fitted construction delay model (structurally I_i x D_i with lognormal D_i); supports offering lognormal as an alternative to Beta-PERT for delay magnitude.

### B26 - Flyvbjerg (2006)
- Title: From Nobel Prize to project management: getting risks right
- Journal: Project Management Journal 37(3), pp. 5-15
- DOI: 10.1177/875697280603700302 (verified via CrossRef)
- Access: FT of the author's arXiv copy (arXiv:1302.3642, 32 pages). Licence NC.
- Context: transportation infrastructure projects (not brick masonry); optimism bias and reference class forecasting.
- Key statements with numbers: average cost inaccuracy in constant prices is 44.7% for rail, 33.8% for bridges and tunnels and 20.4% for roads; no improvement over 70 years; psychological explanation = optimism bias (Kahneman & Tversky; "planning fallacy", Lovallo & Kahneman 2003) versus strategic misrepresentation; reference class forecasting (three steps: select reference class, establish its distribution, position the project) is the "outside view" cure. "40% of projects have a maximum cost overrun of 10%; 80% a maximum overrun of 32%" (roads).
- Limitations: cost overruns for large transport projects; no schedule data; the optimism findings are secondary evidence for any single-activity model.
- Decision supported: expert three-point ranges are likely optimistic; an uplift or a wider upper tail for delay magnitudes is defensible; report high percentiles (P80-P90) as decision values.

## 6. Recommended defensible baseline (only where evidence supports; INFERENCE marked)

1. Risk-event structure: for each risk i, occurrence I_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i) and a three-point delay D_i given occurrence, summed on top of the baseline duration. Precedent: B18 (Beta-PERT delays x Bernoulli occurrence, construction case), B23 and B24 (Bernoulli events; triangular/uniform impacts), B08 (events plus latent variability). Percentiles: report P50/P80/P90/P95; B23 states P80 is common in project management and P95 in finance, B24 reads P80/P90 contingencies from the table. P95 is the hardest to estimate [B05].
2. Delay distribution default: Beta-PERT with lambda = 4 (alpha + beta = 6, mode = elicited most-likely value; mean (a + 4m + b)/6). Supported as the classical family by B01, B04 and used in a construction case by B18. INFERENCE (my derivation from B01/B04 formulas): alpha = 1 + 4(m - a)/(b - a), beta = 1 + 4(b - m)/(b - a); variance = alpha*beta*(b - a)^2/252, i.e. (b - a)^2/28 for a central mode, larger than the classical (b - a)^2/36 (this is B01's factor 9/7). The symmetric member with mean and variance both exactly PERT is alpha = beta = 4 [B04], which corresponds to lambda = 6 in the mode-preserving form (INFERENCE). No verified source calibrates lambda; proposed sensitivity set lambda in {2, 4, 6} is INFERENCE.
   - Robustness switch: report the P80/P90/P95 spread under (a) Beta-PERT, (b) triangular, (c) lognormal matched to the same mean and spread, (d) beta-rectangular mixture [B04]. Why: PERT gives thin tails and low cv [B02, B03, B04]; field data show cv 0.37 to above 2 [B03]; lognormal is supported for activity-duration variability [B02, B11, B08] and a lognormal-impact delay mixture is fitted to construction schedules [B25]. Which alternative is right for masonry delays is NOT established.
3. Continuous productivity variability versus discrete events: keep them as separate terms, T = T0 x (latent variability factor) + sum I_i D_i [structure from B08, B23, B24]. INFERENCE for double counting: word each elicited D_i as the extra delay caused by the event beyond normal productivity variation, and calibrate the latent factor (e.g. lognormal CV) from routine variability only; no verified paper formalises this.
4. Uncertain p_i (qualitative levels): map each Likert/qualitative level to a probability band, not a point, and state the bands [B23, B24: e.g. VL 0-5%, L 5-12%, M 12-20%, H 20-35%, VH 35-100%, project-specific; B21 shows word-to-number values differ by domain]. B23/B24 draw p uniformly inside the band. INFERENCE (law of total probability): if p is redrawn every iteration and each risk can occur once per iteration, P(I = 1) = E[p], so the output distribution equals the model with p fixed at the band midpoint; the band matters only if (a) p is held fixed in an outer loop to show percentile uncertainty, or (b) one draw of p is shared across risks (common cause). No verified paper covers nested Monte Carlo, so treat the two-stage design as a reporting feature and label it INFERENCE.
5. Elicitation and aggregation: pool experts' distributions linearly (equal weights unless seed questions exist); do not average quantiles or use harmonic weighting [B07: rejected as statistically accurate at 5% in 18 of 33 studies]. Equal weights are a defensible default with few experts and few seed questions [B16, B17, B22]; performance weights need seed questions and lose accuracy out of sample [B07]. Delphi in construction typically uses 2-3 rounds, 8-20 experts, mean/median feedback, and Likert ratings [B19]. Structured elicitation protocol and named biases [B22]. Warn that three-point ranges are usually too narrow and forecasts optimistic [B02, B03 citing Tversky & Kahneman; B26: reference-class uplifts for transport projects, e.g. 15% at a 50% acceptable overrun risk and 45% at 10% for roads] - evidence for masonry itself is NR.
6. Risk-matrix scores as inputs: treat the matrix as an ordinal screening device [B27] and check that Monte Carlo ranking agrees; in B23 the two rankings differed (duration ranks: matrix R2 = 1, R3 = 2, R5 = 3 versus Monte Carlo R3 = 1, R5 = 2, R2 = 3, and the paper attributes part of the difference to whether the affected activity is critical - a network effect that does not apply to a single activity, where the ranking should follow p x E[D] more closely; that last clause is INFERENCE).
7. Iterations and precision: report a distribution-free (binomial/order-statistic) CI for each reported percentile [B05, eq. 60]; choose N by a stated tolerance, remembering that tail percentiles need far more runs than central ones [B05, B06]; check convergence by re-running with different seeds or a stopping rule on the mean with a minimum run count [B08: stability 0.1 time units, minimum 3000 runs]. Published project-risk precedents use 10,000 [B15, B20's correlation-uncertainty simulation] and 20,000 [B23] iterations; B15's "convergence at 10,000" is asserted, not shown. INFERENCE: default N = 10,000, increased automatically until the P90/P95 CI half-width meets the tolerance; SE(q_p) = sqrt(p(1 - p)/n)/f(q_p) follows from B05 eqs. (16) and (24), with f estimated from the simulated density. Simple random sampling is adequate; LHS gave about 2x lower MSE for quantiles in one example [B05, Table 1] and is optional (INFERENCE that gains for Bernoulli inputs are unproven).
8. Sensitivity: Spearman rank correlation/SRRC tornado as the primary ranking, always with the fit statistic (R^2 of ranks) and the statement that rank measures assume a near-monotone relation [B10, B09]; SRC and Sobol'-Jansen ranked identically in a project case [B09]; Sobol' cost is (d + 2)n runs and about 10^4 calls for a +/-10% index [B09, B10]. Criticality-type indices [B08, B13] apply only to networks. INFERENCE (elementary algebra): for T = T0 + sum I_i D_i with independent terms, Var(T) = sum Var(I_i D_i) with Var(I_i D_i) = p_i(sigma_i^2 + mu_i^2) - p_i^2 mu_i^2, giving an exact variance-share check on the simulated ranking. Validate a ranking by fixing the low-ranked input and re-simulating [B09]. "Correlation is not causation": state as a limitation (no verified source).
9. Dependency: model risk chains with conditional trigger probabilities [B08 eqs. 2-3] and add impacts by default, with optional reducing/increasing scenarios (B08's 0.3/0.4/0.3 mix and lambda = 0.5 are experimental settings, not evidence). Represent co-movement of durations by a shared multiplicative factor [B02, B03: Y_j = B X_j]. Independence can badly understate variability: SD of total cost was 30% too low under independence in one construction cost dataset [B20; data from Wall 1997, secondary]. Correlation strength on a 3-level scale (0.15/0.45/0.8) with an uncertainty band is a published construction precedent [B20]. Copulas: no verified support (INFERENCE only if adopted).
10. Aleatory/epistemic labelling: type each risk input as aleatoric (variability, e.g. delay magnitude), stochastic (event occurrence) or epistemic (p or impact known only as a band) as in B24; B22 frames the prior as epistemic and the outcome model as aleatory.
