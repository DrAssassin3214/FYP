## 3 Study design
- Type: cross-sectional expert survey plus explainable machine learning (XGBoost regression with SHAP). Organisational risk-management maturity (Generic Risk Maturity Model, GRMM) is used to predict how delay risks are weighted. Not a delay-cause survey of site conditions.
- Country/setting: Australia, construction organisations (contractors, consultants, clients, subcontractors; Tier 1-3; small/medium/large). Sector mix: infrastructure 56%, commercial 21%, industrial 10%, residential 5%, other 8% (p. 9).
- Sample: survey sent to 78 professionals; 62 responses (79.5%); 48 complete questionnaires retained, 14 excluded (p. 9). Maturity classes of the 48: Level 1 = 1 (2.1%); Level 2 = 27 (56.3%); Levels 3-4 combined = 20 (41.7%) (p. 9).
- Respondents: purposive sample of experienced senior managerial, technical, project-management, planning and risk roles (p. 9).
- Scales: maturity rated on a four-point scale (Level 1 Initial/Ad Hoc; 2 Repeatable; 3 Managed; 4 Optimised), 25 criteria across 5 dimensions: G1 management commitment and leadership, G2 organisational risk culture, G3 risk identification, G4 risk analysis, G5 standardised risk management processes (pp. 4, 9-10).
- Delay factors: 22 factors from a PRISMA review of 92 studies (103 global factors, ref [10]), localised by semi-structured interviews with 11 senior Australian professionals (March-July 2024; 55 global factors evaluated in interviews) (p. 10). Impact and probability weights obtained with Fuzzy Best-Worst Method (FBWM)/BWM per respondent; composite risk = impact weight x probability weight (Eq. 1, p. 12) (pp. 10-12). Exact Likert/BWM scale details NR.
- Analysis: normalised weights/ranks per maturity level (Tables 1-2); 66 XGBoost regressions (22 factors x impact, probability, composite risk), five predictors G1-G5; final hyperparameters max depth 3, learning rate 0.05, 100 estimators, gamma 0.1, reg_alpha 0.01, reg_lambda 5; 5-fold cross-validation; SHAP for interpretation; Python 3.14.6, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0 (pp. 14-16).
- Model fit as printed: cross-validated R2 range 0.821 to 0.932, mean 0.892 (p. 16).
- Correlation among G1-G5: all positive, 0.558 to 0.803; G4-G5 r = 0.803; G3-G5 r = 0.736; G2-G4 r = 0.641; G1-G2 r = 0.615; G1-G3 r = 0.614 (p. 18).
- Ethics: QUT HREC protocol LR 2024-7852-19920, approved 16 August 2024 (p. 30).
- Hypotheses: H1 prioritisation differs across maturity levels; H2 maturity dimensions are differentially associated with evaluation of individual delay risks (p. 3). No formal significance test of H1/H2 is reported in the text.

## 5 Relevance to risk identification for brick masonry in India (plain, no overclaiming)
- Useful as a worked example of a delay-factor register of 22 factors scored by separate impact and probability weights and combined as impact x probability, which is the same structure as a P x I risk matrix. Method precedent only.
- Several of its 22 factors overlap with masonry-activity delay causes that an Indian risk register would also contain: Shortage of labour, Labor Productivity, Mistakes during the execution of works, Unclear and inadequate details in drawings, Rainfall and Weather conditions, Delay in subcontractors work, Poor project management and supervision, Injuries on Site. The paper gives their weights and ranks (Tables 1-2) but only for general Australian construction, not masonry.
- Notable for this project: labour shortage and labour productivity rank low on composite risk in this Australian sample (ranks 11-18 and 12-17 across maturity levels), which should not be read as applicable to Indian brick-masonry labour conditions.
- Supports the idea that risk rankings vary with organisational context and that rankings from one country should be recalibrated before use elsewhere (the paper says this itself, pp. 3, 27).
- It is an AI/ML (XGBoost + SHAP) study of how organisational maturity affects risk weighting, not a rule-based delay predictor and not a masonry study; the XGBoost-SHAP part is not directly usable for a risk-identification register.
- Not evidence about Indian site conditions, brickwork productivity, or masonry-specific risks.

## 6 Limitations
Authors' stated (pp. 28-29): single national/industry context (Australia), purposive sample not designed for generalisation; relatively small sample; four-point ordinal maturity scale treated as continuous so SHAP patterns are directional only; expert judgement subjectivity; cross-sectional design.
Observed by this reviewer (from printed content): only 48 organisation-level observations for 66 models with 5 predictors, and only 1 respondent at Level 1 and the Level 3 and 4 groups merged, so maturity-level comparisons for L1 rest on n = 1; CV R2 values of 0.821-0.932 on n = 48 may be optimistic and no held-out test set is reported; the study describes maturity as influencing risk perception (expert weights) rather than actual delay occurrence, and no actual project delay data are used; weights are normalised (relative within each column), so they show relative priority, not absolute delay duration; no significance tests for H1/H2 are reported; text/table mismatch noted above (p. 15 vs Table 2); some intro text on factor localisation is repeated; paper date is 2026 so it is recent, with limited citation history.
