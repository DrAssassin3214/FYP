## 3 Study design
- Type: field observational study, Activity Analysis (Construction Industry Institute method; extension of work sampling) on 5 case-study projects, plus sensitivity/regression/correlation analysis of the observations (p.1, 4-5).
- Country: Pakistan, mainly federal (northern) region (p.4).
- Projects (n = 5): semi high-rise commercial buildings, all more than 50% constructed at time of data collection (p.4-5). Observed tasks: block masonry, plastering, formwork, steel fixing, concrete pouring (p.5, p.6). Task type was not separated in the results; categories are generic (p.6).
- Respondents: none (no questionnaire respondents). Labour observed directly by the authors, who personally recorded on-site observations (p.4). Minimum labour set at 400 per project (p.5). Standard Gouett et al. [4] forms modified.
- Observation protocol: 3 rounds per hour, tally marks per category; 8 working hours/day for more than 5 consecutive days per project (p.5-6).
- Categories: seven (direct work; preparatory work and instructions; tools and equipment; material handling; waiting; traveling; personal), grouped as Direct, Support, Delay per CII (p.2, 9).
- Scale: none (counts of observations, percentages).
- Sample size: multinomial formula (Thompson); 95% confidence, d = 0.05, m = 3, n = 510 observations per study period; 8 periods per day, 4,080 observations required (p.7).
- Analysis tools: weighted averages; regression and correlation of direct work with each other activity via MS Excel and @RISK (all 54,850 data points); ANOVA p-values (p.9-10).
- Validation: results shared with field professionals on the case-study projects who validated the observations (p.11); no formal procedure described.

## 5 Relevance to brick-masonry risk identification in India
- Gives a measured, time-use view of labour on Pakistani building sites in which block masonry was one of five tasks: pooled direct work 35.08%, support 37.25%, delay 27.67% (Table 6, p.9). It does not isolate masonry, so none of these values can be attributed to brick masonry alone.
- Names site-level causes of lost direct time that can be listed as candidate risk factors for a masonry activity (all stated by the authors as observations or discussion, not ranked by RII): poor planning and supervision, supervising staff dismissal after financial crisis, inexperienced/subcontracted labour, poor site layout and long travel distance, missing facilities at the work area, poor tool management, poor material handling/placement, waiting caused by poor activity planning and crew balance, late starts/early quits around lunch and end of day (pp.7-11).
- Gives a method (activity analysis, categories, sample-size rule) that could inform a field-observation component; it is not a risk-ranking or RII study and has no India data.
- Use only as supporting evidence for the idea that labour-time loss categories (waiting, material handling, travel, tools, supervision) are plausible risk factors; any ranking must come from the project's own survey.

## 6 Limitations
- Five projects, all commercial semi-high-rise buildings in northern Pakistan; observations not separated by task, so no masonry-specific results (p.5-6).
- Authors' own observation, one observer group; observer effect, inter-rater consistency and training not discussed. Sample of days "more than 5" per project; exact days not stated (p.5-6).
- Some project details and data "expurgated" for confidentiality (p.6).
- Printed inconsistencies (my arithmetic on printed values): Table 2 project totals sum to 55529 whereas Table 5 grand total is 54850; Table 3 average direct work 34.4 vs Table 6 pooled 35.08 (authors explain weighted vs plain averaging, p.8, but the two tables still differ).
- Statistical interpretation is questionable: all ANOVA p-values are above 0.05 (Table 8, 0.13 to 0.58) yet the authors say this "statistically validate[s]" correlation (p.10). Regression sign/ranking treats "personal" as most influential on direct work with correlation 0.585 that the authors themselves call weak. Observation counts across categories within an hour are compositional, so negative/positive correlations are partly arithmetic. The unit of the regression/correlation (observations vs hours vs projects) is not stated.
- Validation by field professionals is mentioned in one sentence with no detail (p.11).
- No productivity output metric (quantity per labour-hour) is measured; "productivity" here means direct-work time share.
- Claims of generalisability to similar countries (p.12) are not supported by the sample.
