## 3 Study design
- Type: quantitative questionnaire survey (face-to-face interview, self-administered answers); Spearman correlation plus binary logistic regression (p.3-4).
- Country: Victoria, Australia (p.3).
- n: 39 contractors / 39 multistory building projects completed 2011-2016 (Abstract; p.3).
- Respondents: contractors drawn from the Victorian Dept of Treasury and Finance prequalified list (p.3). Individual respondent roles NR.
- Productivity measure: PF = actual productivity / planned productivity, with productivity = project cost / duration (Eq. 1, p.4). Assumes fixed-price contracts and no significant cost variation (p.4).
- Scale: relative importance of each practice rated 1-5 (not important to extremely important); RII = (5n5+4n4+3n3+2n2+n1)/(5(n5+n4+n3+n2+n1)) (Eq. 2, p.4). Implementation levels 0-5 per practice.
- 11 HRM practices: crew composition, skill assessment, employee training, career development, nonfinancial incentives, financial incentives, social activities, stability of organizational structure, clear delegation of responsibility, retention plan for experienced personnel, exit interviews.
- Methods: EPV check (>10), baseline PF cut-offs (0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05), 3-fold and 4-fold cross-validation, ROC/AUC, bootstrap with 1,000 samples, SPSS-24 (p.4, 6).

### Literature statements quoted as numbers (secondary, from cited studies, p.2)
Wang et al. (2010): craft training raised productivity 5%, cut turnover 10%, cut absenteeism 2.5%. Fagbenle et al. (2004): bricklayers receiving nonfinancial incentives had significantly improved productive time (Nigeria). These are cited, not findings of this study.

## 5 Relevance to brick-masonry risk identification in India
- Supports a risk-factor group for the register: poor crew composition and no skill assessment, lack of training, unstable/unclear site organisation and responsibility delegation, weak incentives and retention, as management-side contributors to low productivity and delay. This is indirect evidence from Australian building projects.
- Delay vs HRM correlation (rs -0.30, p 0.06) is weak and not significant at 5%; delay vs stability of organizational structure (-0.47) and social activity (-0.41) are the significant items. Can be cited only as an association.
- The RII formula (Eq. 2) is the same type of calculation used for ranking risks; the ranking of practices (0.836 down to 0.574) is a useful example of RII reporting. Only reported as a ranking of HRM practices, not masonry risks.
- Masonry/bricklayers are named only in the literature review (crew trades listed p.3; Fagbenle et al. on bricklayers, p.2). The study does not isolate masonry or brickwork.
- Cannot be used as Indian evidence or as a masonry productivity rate.

## 6 Limitations
- Project-level productivity (cost/time ratio), not task-level masonry output; assumes planned duration accurate and no cost variation (p.4).
- Small sample (39 projects, 26 used in model building and 13 validation per fold); multistory buildings from prequalified Victorian contractors only.
- Binary outcome depends on chosen cut-off (0.90); other cut-offs produce different models (Tables 4-6). Model AUC on validation 0.67 only; bootstrap SE very large (18.708 for beta).
- Correlation not causation; Table 2 delay result has p = 0.06; significance footnote symbols printed as "p > 0.05".
- Practices were rated for importance but respondent counts per RII not shown; HRM only, no material, weather, or design factors.
- Single-predictor model (HRM total score) only.
