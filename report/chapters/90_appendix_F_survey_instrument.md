# Appendix F. Proposed expert-survey instrument

> **PROPOSED, NOT ADMINISTERED.** This instrument has not been sent to anyone. No response exists, no result in this report comes from it, and no number in this report should be read as survey evidence. It is the instrument the team proposes to use in the next phase (Chapter 8). Item wording and scales follow the committee's domain-expert review (2026-10-08, "Expert survey and site data form"); the statistical plan follows the methods review of the same date. Both are the team's own working documents. Ethics approval and the institution's consent requirements must be confirmed before any distribution (not yet sought).

## F.1 Purpose and design

The aim is to obtain the team's own, masonry-specific evidence on how often each of 15 delay causes slows brick or block masonry on Indian sites and how severe the slip is when it happens. Frequency and severity are asked separately because a published RII mixes them, and neither is a probability or a number of days. The results would be labelled Expert Judgment, with n stated, and would sit beside the literature seed; they would not be converted into probabilities or days. The 15 items were chosen to cover the library's activity-level risks that a site engineer can recognise and that the committee judged most likely to stop a masonry gang. A 46-item survey would invite fatigue and was rejected.

## F.2 Introduction shown to respondents (proposed text)

"This survey is part of a student final-year project at [institution] on delay risks in brick and block masonry. It takes about 10 minutes. Participation is voluntary and anonymous: we do not ask for your name, your employer's name or any project name, and you may stop at any time. Answers are used only in the project report and any paper derived from it, and are reported in groups. Please think of the masonry (brick or block wall) activities that you personally supervised or managed in the last 3 years. There are no right or wrong answers."

## F.3 Section A: respondent profile (all choices from a list; no free text except where stated)

1. Current role: site / project engineer; project manager; contractor or proprietor; labour or masonry sub-contractor; QA/QC; client or project-management consultant; faculty with site experience.
2. Total years in construction (bands).
3. Years directly supervising masonry (bands). Respondents with less than 1 year of masonry supervision are excluded from the analysis (pre-specified).
4. Number of building projects with masonry supervised (bands).
5. Building types (multiple choice): low-rise residential; high-rise residential; commercial; institutional or government.
6. Unit types used (multiple choice): burnt clay brick; fly-ash brick; AAC; concrete block.
7. State(s) or region of the sites.
8. Employer type: developer; general contractor; labour contractor; government / PWD; consultant.
9. How masons were mostly employed: through a labour contractor / piece-rate; daily wage directly.

## F.4 Section B: the 15 items, two ratings each

For every item the respondent sees the same two questions. The frame above the section reads: "Think of the masonry activities you personally supervised or managed in the last 3 years."

**Frequency** (how often it caused delay to masonry):

| Code | Label |
|---|---|
| 1 | Never or almost never |
| 2 | Occasionally |
| 3 | Sometimes |
| 4 | Often |
| 5 | On almost every masonry activity |
| N/A | Not applicable / no experience |

**Severity** (when it occurs, its effect on the masonry finish date):

| Code | Label |
|---|---|
| 1 | No noticeable slip |
| 2 | Absorbed within the week |
| 3 | Finish date slips a little |
| 4 | Finish date slips noticeably |
| 5 | Holds up following trades (plaster, flooring) as well |
| DK | Don't know |

The anchor wording is the team's own scale and is an Assumption. It is never converted to days or to a probability in the report.

## F.5 Section C: closing items

(a) "From the list, pick the three causes that most often delayed masonry on your sites." This gives a ranking check that is independent of the Likert items.

(b) Open text: "Any other cause of masonry delay you have seen?" This feeds the completeness of the library and is the only free-text field.

(c) Optional, for items 1, 3, 7 and 8 only: "Of the last 10 masonry activities you supervised, in about how many did this happen?" A count may later be entered for a risk as Expert Judgment with n stated; it is never labelled Literature.

(d) One instructed-response (attention-check) item placed inside Section B: "For this row, please select 'Occasionally' for frequency." Respondents who fail it are excluded (pre-specified).

## F.6 Bias controls

- Literature ranks, the tool and its matrix are not shown before or during the survey.
- Item order is randomised per respondent; frequency and severity are separate questions.
- "Not applicable" and "Don't know" are offered; they are never imputed (pairwise deletion).
- Item wording is neutral (no blame words such as "indiscipline"); recall is anchored to a time window and to activities the respondent personally supervised.
- Contractor-side and client / consultant-side respondents are both recruited and reported separately (each side tends to rate the other side's causes higher; this is a hypothesis, Expert Judgment).
- Anonymity; team members do not answer; the wording is piloted with 2 to 3 site engineers before release.

## F.7 Pre-specified analysis plan

The plan is to be dated and saved in the repository before any response arrives. All items below are Derived Calculations from Expert Judgment ratings and would be reported with n.

1. Per item: number answering, median and interquartile range; frequency index (FI) and severity index (SI) with the same RII formula as the seed studies on the 1 to 5 scale; importance index FI x SI (the structure used by A11, an index the seed excludes); respondent-bootstrap 95% intervals (5,000 resamples) and bootstrap rank intervals.
2. Kendall's W across respondents (complete cases, tie-corrected) for frequency and for severity, and per group when a group has at least 8 respondents, with a permutation p-value. W measures consensus on the order, not the reliability of the instrument.
3. Reliability: Cronbach's alpha (Cronbach, 1951) with a confidence interval for convention only (the 15 causes are a checklist, not one construct, so a high alpha mostly reflects respondents who rate everything high); split-half reliability of the item order (1,000 random splits of respondents, Spearman-Brown corrected) is the reliability that matters.
4. Group differences per item (contractor side vs client / consultant side) by Mann-Whitney with Holm correction over the 15 items, with a rank-biserial effect size, only if each group has at least 10 respondents.
5. Comparison with the literature seed by Spearman correlation on the items with a pre-registered one-to-one mapping (about 10 of 15), exact p-value, described as exploratory. At n = 10 only a correlation of about 0.65 or more (two-sided 5%) could be detected, which is stated in advance.
6. Optional short extra section, kept separate from the 15 items so that the main survey stays at about 10 minutes: respondents are shown the 22 site-fact rule conditions (Appendix C) and say for each whether it is a sensible warning sign (yes / no / unsure). This would endorse the conditions only; it would produce no thresholds and no numbers.

## F.8 Sample-size honesty

The sample would be purposive and snowball (faculty, alumni, contractor contacts), so no population margin of error is claimed. With a planning standard deviation of one Likert point (Assumption AS-18, close to the 0.96 that M01's printed counts give), the half-width of one item's index is about 0.10 at n = 15 and 0.07 at n = 30, whereas adjacent seed risks differ by 0.001 to 0.02. Ten to fifteen responses are therefore a pilot; the target is at least 30, with the response rate (invited versus completed) and the respondent profile reported. For comparison, the seed surveys had N = 44 (M01), 201 (M06) and 163 (A14).
